# Space Whiskers: The Holy Cow: notes

**The game.** The remake of Space Mouse, made fresh: a side-view platformer about Pip, a mouse astronaut, in a universe run by cats, on a
quest tied to Stellamoo, the Holy Cow. This folder is its working folder (the owner, 2026-11-16). The game so far is **a test level**: Pip
walks, runs and jumps through a corner of the shipyard with his approved animations, so they can be judged in motion. It is built but unlisted
(`"listed": false`): open it at `/games/spacewhiskers/` on the site (behind the login), or `npm run serve` locally.

**The scale (the owner's rule).** "I really want the resolution of the game to be what these images are. I want that to be the scale of the
whole game." The grid of the sprite sheet `ref/pip.png` is the game's: one of its art pixels (2.86 picture pixels) is one game pixel, for
every sprite, prop and tile alike. Pip stands **49 game pixels** tall and is drawn and shown 1:1 (k .5, not halved as Space Mouse's Pip is).
The **screen is 480 x 270** game pixels (the owner: "a notch bigger. We don't have to follow SNES 1:1, but still keep the screen small"): 16:9,
a quarter of 1080p each way, so every game pixel is exactly 4 x 4 screen pixels on a 1080p monitor (8 x 8 on 4K); Pip is a fifth of its height
(5.5 of him tall). The sprite sheet itself is about 380 x 213 game pixels (384 x 216 was the first idea). Written into `art/style/style.json`.
The level picture `ref/levelinspiration.png` is a **zoomed-out overview** (the owner, 2026-11-16: "the level inspiration should be seen as
a bit zoomed out. The actual gameplay should be a smaller frame"): Pip is about 70 of its pixels tall, so one 480 x 270 game screen covers about
686 x 386 of it, about two thirds of its width and height. Levels are drawn at the sprite sheet's scale; the picture shows what they look
like, not how much of them fits on a screen.

## The Bubbling Moon (chapter 9): design and art

`story/BUBBLING_MOON.md` is the level design proposal: what the review notes decided, a hook (the moon has hiccups: one beat drives bubbles, curd, pipes and lights), the route in five
zones, ten puzzles to pick from (shiny means thin, bounce on the beat, a pit of bubbles, which hole, the curd ferry, valves in order, the pipe slide, the box with Miss Clou in it, four torn
map pieces, curing the hiccup), the scrapbook photos, the open questions, and the art plan (which sheet holds what, how the layers scroll). The sheets are in `ref/bubblingmoon_*.png`
(8 pictures, 3840 x 2160, one art pixel = 4 x 4 picture pixels, so a screen is 1920 x 1080 and Pip 196 tall), flat magenta ground, labels under the items. The five props and foreground sheets are **cut into
291 single sprites** in `ref/frames/bubblingmoon_*` (`tools/cut_sheets.py`; none snapped to the game grid yet); `story/BUBBLING_MOON_LAYOUTS.md` has the five zones with layout sketches and the sprites each uses, and
`story/BUBBLING_MOON_DECISIONS.md` what the review notes say. `python3 games/spacewhiskers/tools/pack_moon.py out.zip` builds the pack zip.

## The test level

**The view.** One detail level (RES 2: a cell is a game pixel, `PX = RES / 2` = 1). `fitView` (main.js) picks a whole number of device pixels per
cell so the view is about `screenWidth` x 9/16 of it: 1920 x 1080 gives 472 x 270 (the page's frame takes a little), a 1366 x 768 laptop 444 x 256.
The cell size is rounded, not floored, so a window between two sizes stays near the agreed screen rather than growing to 600+. The wheel and pinch
zoom are off. A phone held upright shows a tall narrow view (179 x 422): turn it.

**The map** (world.js, `levelRows`): 180 x 40 tiles of 8 game pixels (`tile: 4` base units), four screens of shipyard with a roof and end walls.
A: crates and the first catwalk; B: a raised deck and a pit; C: the scaffold tower in front of the big window (four catwalks, 6 tiles = 48 pixels
apart, climbed by jumping through them); D: a long run over two crates to the great cheese. Ten cheese crumbs. `=` is a one-way catwalk (Down drops
through), `|` a scaffold upright on the back wall. The window (`WINDOW`) leaves the back wall bare so the stars and the planet (drawn on the sky
layer, `skyCell`) show.

**Pip** (pip.js) is a `Character` 18 x 46 pixels. His normal pace is the **run** drawings at 150 px/s (three body heights a second); Shift or X sprints at 215;
C (or a gentle push of a stick) walks at 70 with the walk drawings, which are pinned to a stride of 4.5 pixels a frame and so cannot go faster without the boots sliding. He reaches speed
in 0.07 s and stops in 0.05 s. Gravity comes from the two numbers a player feels: `jumpHeight` (80 px, one and two thirds Pips) and `jumpTime` (0.34 s to the top); falling is 1.55x faster,
air control .9. The drawings:
- **run and walk** step by the distance covered (`runStride` 10, `walkStride` 4.5 pixels a frame), so the boots keep pace with the floor; below 100 px/s the walk is shown, above it the run;
- **idle** at 6 fps on the clock, below 8 px/s;
- **jump**: `jump_1` for 0.07 s at take-off, then `jump_2..6` by vertical speed (rising fast, rising, the top, falling, falling fast), `jump_7` for 0.1 s on landing only if he has stopped
  (landing at speed goes straight to the run); `jump_0` (the crouch before the take-off) is not used, since the jump leaves at once.

**The camera** (main.js, `swCamera`): locked to Pip on whole cells, no easing of the engine's (`cameraEase: 1000`, `cameraTop: 0`): the left edge is his whole-cell position minus a look-ahead of
about 30 cells the way he runs (eased in over half a second, held while he stands), the top edge settles on the floor he stands on (64% down the view) and is clamped so his feet stay between
20% and 86% of the view's height: a jump does not bob the screen, a long fall or a high jump moves it.

**The look and the lights** (lights.js). The materials paint fixed palettes, the sheet's way (the wall's have six steps, so pools of light read as bands). Low ambient light; every lamp is a cone
or a small round light with shadows: ceiling lamps on cables (downward cones), a pulsing red alarm lamp over the pit and a blue lamp inside the scaffold, Pip's helmet lamp (a cone that turns with him),
a glow round the helmet and the great cheese. Pip blocks lamp light (`markSpriteDyn`, `blocksLamps`), so lamps throw his shadow on the back wall. **Test lights**: click drops a light (up to 4, the
oldest goes), 1 to 4 choose warm, cold, spot (a cone straight down) or alarm (pulsing), F a light that follows the mouse, H the helmet lamp, K clears, L drops one above Pip, right click takes the
nearest away. A lamp costs about r² x its angle in ray steps and the hall is open, so keep lamps small and conical: see `docs/LESSONS.md` ("A scrolling platformer at 480 x 270").

**Frame cost** (headless Chrome, software GL, 1920 x 1080; a real machine is faster, F3 shows its own): standing about 8 ms; a moving frame 16 to 23 ms, most of it the engine's pass over the
127,000 cells of the view and the lamps recast as the camera scrolls. `python3 tests/perf.py spacewhiskers` prints the stages. The knob is `screenWidth`.

**What is off / next.** The level is grey boxes and orange bars: its art should come from `ref/levelinspirationprops.png` (platforms, girders,
crates, machines) cut and snapped like Pip's frames. Pip has no slide, crouch, wall slide, landing dust or sounds of his own yet (the sheet has the
drawings for slide, crouch and wall slide). The lamp pools show their bands (the palettes' steps). The engine redoes the whole view every moving frame; a scroll-shifted terrain cache would help
every game (ROADMAP, Performance). The walk is rarely seen at the new pace (the C key, a stick): its stride could be redrawn longer if a slow pace matters. Then the Cat, and the story's first level.

## Animations

How they are drafted, and why, is in `docs/ANIMATION.md`. In short: the owner's drawings are the approved keys; models draft the frames
between them (Wan 2.7 between two keys, ToonCrafter through all of them) or redraw the base sprite from the rig (PixelLab); the tools snap
every frame to the game grid and palette, register it (feet on the ground, helmet over the idle's) and measure it; the owner picks.

**The workflow for each animation**: pose the keys (`art/anims/<anim>.json`), look at `bakeoff/<anim>/guides/rig.gif` (written by
`node tools/art/bakeoff.mjs spacewhiskers <anim> --dry`), the owner approves the motion (`"rigApproved": true`), then PixelLab draws it
(`--methods pixellab-skeleton`); the drawing methods refuse an unapproved rig. Approved (the owner, 2026-11-16: "I accept all of them for
now, maybe we will iterate later"): the walk, the run, the idle and the jump, all in the game.
